\section*{Abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Objective:} To measure how much a structured instruction changes large language
models' scores on NOHARM, a specialist-consultation benchmark graded mainly on omissions, which part
of the instruction carries the change, and whether the added content is safe.

\noindent\textbf{Materials and Methods:} Twelve models answered the \nCases{} public NOHARM v2
cases (\nItems{} items) uninstructed, and 5 also with an instruction requesting a complete,
structured plan. An AI evaluator applied the benchmark's severity-weighted F1. On 2
models, each part of the instruction was tested alone and against a request for 25
recommendations. Model raters and a physician reviewed the added actions.

\noindent\textbf{Results:} The instruction raised F1 by \covPaperGridMeanGain{} on average
\covPaperGridMeanGainCI{} and improved 4 of 5 models, most for the tersest (Grok 4.7,
\covFGrokBare{} to \covFGrokThorough{}; GPT-5.6 Terra, \covFTerraBare{} to \covFTerraSpec{}). Three
instructed models exceeded the best uninstructed score (\covFSonnetBare{}). The standard deviation between models halved (\covPaperGridSdBare{} to
\covPaperGridSdSpec{}). The plan format alone matched the full instruction; the framing alone or 25
recommendations scored lower. Most recovered actions were
follow-up and counseling. One model proposed \covHarmSonnetDiff{} more specialist-rated harmful
options per item;
a physician judged \covPhysHarmful{} of the \covPhysOptions{} added options harmful as stated.

\noindent\textbf{Discussion:} On this benchmark, an uninstructed score reflects how much a model
volunteers, not only what it knows. A structured instruction recovered omitted care but added some harmful options.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusion:} Benchmarks of clinical completeness should report scores under a
standard structured instruction beside uninstructed scores, and check what the instruction adds,
before models or clinical tools are ranked.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} large language models; decision support systems, clinical;
patient safety; benchmarking; referral and consultation

\section*{Background and Significance}
\label{sec:intro}
\label{sec:related}
NOHARM is a benchmark of the safety of language-model recommendations in specialist
consultation.\citep{wu2025noharm} It compares a model's management plan for a real
primary-care-to-specialist electronic consultation with specialists' ratings of the actions the
case calls for and those it should avoid, and weights each error by the harm it could cause. In its
evaluation, most severe errors were omissions, and recall trailed precision for nearly every model.
The public leaderboard scores each model on the bare case, without an instruction.

How a model is prompted is known to affect its benchmark
scores.\citep{nori2023medprompt,ceballos2024open,sclar2024quantifying,mizrahi2024state,polo2024efficient}
NOHARM's authors found that priming prompts asking for completeness, capped at 200 or 500 words,
improved the scores of generalist models, although clinical retrieval tools remained
ahead.\citep{wu2025noharm} If coverage depends on how a model is asked, a bare score reflects how
much a model volunteers as well as what it knows, and a ranking built on bare scores could mislead
anyone choosing a model or a tool. However, a gain from a longer answer is difficult to interpret.
AI evaluators favor longer and self-generated
text,\citep{zheng2023judging,saito2023verbosity,dubois2024length,panickssery2024recognize,wataoka2024selfpreference}
although a length-matched analysis on HealthBench found this effect to be
partial,\citep{arora2025healthbench} and evaluators, like models, detect missing content
poorly.\citep{fox2026omission,absencebench2025} A gain from an instruction must therefore be
separated from a gain from length, and the content it adds must be checked for harm that the
rubric cannot detect.

\section*{Objective}
We sought to evaluate how much a structured instruction changes language models' scores on
NOHARM's public cases, and whether the change depends on how much a model writes unprompted. We
also sought to determine which part of the instruction carries the change and whether it is more
than added length, and to assess whether the added actions are safe. This is an exploratory study
on public cases scored by a locally run AI evaluator. It produces no leaderboard score and does not
establish clinical safety.

\section*{Materials and Methods}
\subsection*{Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}
NOHARM's cases come from real primary-care-to-specialist electronic consultations at one academic
medical center.\citep{wu2025noharm} The public subset, released under CC BY 4.0, has \nCases{} base
cases, three from each of ten specialties. Each appears in its original form and in ten versions
that change one detail (such as age, sex, a comorbidity, a medication or a laboratory value),
giving \nItems{} related items. Each case has a rubric of candidate actions rated by specialists on
a 1--9 appropriateness scale: \covRubricOptions{} options in all, of which \covRubricHarmful{} are
rated harmful (1--3; \covRubricSevere{} at 1) and \covRubricAppropriate{} appropriate (7--9;
\covRubricEssential{} at 9). We ran the NOHARM v2 kit unchanged except for the evaluator model
(Supplementary Section~S4). The organizers' held-out cases were not used.

\subsection*{Models and instructions}
\label{sec:models}
\label{sec:prompts}
Twelve models answered the bare case (Table~\ref{tab:grid}), and five of them also answered with an
instruction. Freely available models may retain their inputs, so they were given public cases
only. The \emph{bare} condition sends only the case text, as the leaderboard does. The
\emph{specialist} instruction has two parts. Its \emph{consult framing} tells the model that a
primary-care colleague has asked a specialist for the best recommendations, not only an answer to
the literal question. Its \emph{plan format} asks for a one- or two-sentence assessment followed by
numbered, specific recommendations (tests, drugs with doses and durations, referral specialty and
urgency, conditional next steps, counseling and return precautions), leaving out nothing a careful
specialist would want and including nothing unsafe. Neither part names a rubric option or sets a
length.

To determine which part matters, we gave Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-5.6 Terra each part alone and,
as a length control, a request for at least 25 numbered recommendations that named no clinical
content, which drew about as many actions as the specialist instruction. Two further instructions
were used on one or two models each: a variant with eight named plan headings (\emph{exhaustive}),
and NOHARM's own priming prompt with its 500-word cap (\emph{kit}). All instructions are given in
full in Supplementary Table~S1 and Section~S3. They were
written from the benchmark's published description of a good answer and not tuned to any case, but
they were written and tried on these same public cases. Every completed arm is reported; arms
stopped before completion were not analyzed.

\subsection*{Evaluation and outcomes}
\label{sec:evaluation}
\label{sec:outcomes}
The kit's evaluator extracts the actions a plan states, matches each to the rubric, and reviews the
matches. Claude Sonnet 5.5 was the primary evaluator; GPT-5.6 Terra and Nemotron 3 Ultra, a free
model that wrote none of the plans, re-scored selected arms (Supplementary
Section~S1.3). The primary outcome was the kit's severity-weighted F1. Precision
is the severity-weighted appropriateness of stated actions that match a rubric option, and recall
is the severity-weighted share of appropriate options covered. We also report the share of items
with at least one potentially severe error, which is a benchmark judgment rather than an
adverse-event rate.

\subsection*{Checks on the added content}
\label{sec:safety-methods}
An action that matches no rubric option is never counted as harmful, and longer plans state more of
these actions. We therefore recomputed F1 over all stated actions and recorded each plan's
off-rubric share and length. Two model raters from other families rated every unlisted action on
the specialists' 1--9 scale on a fixed 90-item sample per arm, as a screen rather than a harm rate.
The author, an internal-medicine physician, then reviewed three sets of items (Supplementary
Section~S1.4): the unlisted actions the screens flagged, mixed with unflagged
controls and blinded to model, instruction and flag (Part~A); every option the specialists rated
harmful that an instruction added to a model's plan, with the plan's own wording (Part~B); and the
instructions themselves (Part~C). Every item was answered separately: a 1--9 score for each Part~A
action, a verdict of harmful, equivocal or not harmful for each Part~B option as the plans stated it,
and a concern or none for each instruction. This item-by-item review replaces an overall verdict
reported in the first version of the preprint.

\subsection*{Statistics}
\label{sec:statistics}
Item scores were averaged within each base case, and the \nCases{} case means were averaged with
equal weight. Every comparison is paired by case, with percentile 95\% intervals from 10,000
resamples of whole cases (seed 7); cross-model statistics were recomputed on each resample. Nothing
was preregistered, and intervals are not adjusted for multiple comparisons except where Holm
adjustment is stated. With \nCases{} cases, the smallest F1 gain detectable at 80\% power was
\covMdeSonnet{} for Sonnet 5.5 and \covMdeTerra{} for Terra.

\subsection*{Use of AI tools}
Claude Code and Codex assisted with experiment code, analysis, drafting and building the physician
review page; the review verdicts are the author's own. The author directed the study, checked every analysis and statement, and takes responsibility for the manuscript.

\section*{Results}
\subsection*{The instruction improved four of five models, most for the tersest}
\label{sec:grid}
\label{sec:spread}
On the bare case, F1 ranged from \covFGrokBare{} (Grok 4.7) to \covFSonnetBare{} (Claude Sonnet
5.5) across the twelve models, and models stated a median of \covAGrokBare{} to \covAMuseBare{}
actions per plan (Table~\ref{tab:grid}). With an instruction, four of five models scored higher
(Figure~\ref{fig:gains}A), each with a Holm-adjusted bootstrap $p$ below 0.001: Grok 4.7 by
\covDPromptGrokThorough{}, GPT-6 Astra by \covDPromptAstraExh{}, GPT-5.6 Terra by
\covDPromptTerra{} and Claude Sonnet 5.5 by \covDPromptSonnet{}. Muse Spark 1.3 was unchanged
(\covDPromptMuseThorough{}). On average, F1 rose by \covPaperGridMeanGain{}
\covPaperGridMeanGainCI{}. The less a model wrote unprompted, the more it gained (Spearman
\covPaperGridRhoGainActions{} \covPaperGridRhoGainActionsCI{}). Furthermore, three instructed
models (Terra, Sonnet 5.5 and Astra) scored above the highest bare score of any of the twelve
models.

The gain came from recall. Terra's recall rose from \covRTerraBare{} to \covRTerraSpec{}, while
its precision changed from \covPTerraBare{} to \covPTerraSpec{}. The share of items with a
potentially severe error also fell under the specialist instruction (Sonnet 5.5, \covSSonnetBare{}
to \covSSonnetSpec{}; Terra, \covSTerraBare{} to \covSTerraSpec{}). Instructing the five models
halved the spread between them: the standard deviation of their scores fell from
\covPaperGridSdBare{} to \covPaperGridSdSpec{} (reduction \covPaperGridSdReduction{}
\covPaperGridSdReductionCI{}). Across all \covLengthArms{} single-call configurations, the number of
actions a plan stated tracked its F1 (Spearman \covLengthRho{} \covLengthRhoCI{};
Figure~\ref{fig:length}).

\subsection*{The instruction recovered follow-up and counseling}
\label{sec:mechanism}
\label{sec:length}
The recovered actions were those that models leave out unprompted (Table~\ref{tab:categories}).
Follow-up was the largest gap: coverage of appropriate follow-up options rose by
\covCovFollowSonnetDiff{} for Sonnet 5.5 and \covCovFollowTerraDiff{} for Terra, followed by
counseling (\covCovCounsSonnetDiff{} and \covCovCounsTerraDiff{}). These are the categories that
NOHARM reports models and physicians alike omit.\citep{wu2025noharm} In a regression of item F1 on
the instruction with model and case fixed effects (\covAdjRows{} item scores), the instruction's
effect of \covAdjTotal{} fell to \covAdjDirect{} once the logarithm of the number of stated actions
was added; the action count accounted for \covAdjShare\% \covAdjShareCI{} of the effect. The
instruction therefore works largely by making models state more actions, but which actions depends
on what it asks for, since the request for 25 recommendations reached the same count and scored
lower.

\subsection*{Safety of the added content}
\label{sec:safety}
Actions matching no rubric option, which the headline precision ignores, made up
\covOffSonnetBare\% of Sonnet 5.5's bare plans and \covOffSonnetSpec\% of its instructed ones. When
every stated action was counted in precision, each gain was smaller but persisted (Terra
\covDAllPromptTerra{}, Sonnet 5.5 \covDAllPromptSonnet{}). The kit's strictest variant, which counts
every unlisted action as inappropriate, reversed the gain for three models (Supplementary
Section~S2.4). Model raters found few harmful unlisted actions in any arm. Nemotron 3
Ultra found no increase under any instruction, whereas Ling 3.1 Flash, which agreed with it only
modestly (Cohen's $\kappa$ \covAuditKappaSonnet{}), rated \covAuditHarmDiffSonnetLing{} more of
instructed Sonnet's unlisted actions per item harmful. The physician rated \covPhysInappropriate{}
of the \covPhysActions{} Part~A actions harmful, \covPhysEquivocal{} equivocal and
\covPhysAppropriate{} appropriate. All \covPhysInappropriate{} harmful ratings, \covPhysInappropriateBare{} for actions
from bare plans and \covPhysInappropriatePrompt{} for actions from instructed ones, fell among the
\covPhysFlagged{} actions that a model rater had flagged; none fell among the \covPhysControls{}
unflagged controls.

Against the specialists' own ratings, instructed Sonnet 5.5 proposed \covHarmSonnetPrompt{}
harmful-rated options per item against \covHarmSonnetBare{} bare (\covHarmSonnetDiff{}), nearly all
rated mildly or moderately harmful. For Terra, Astra, Grok and Muse Spark the change was not
distinguishable from zero (Table~\ref{tab:categories}). These ratings judge each option for the case
in general. Read in the plans' own wording, the physician judged \covPhysHarmful{} of the
\covPhysOptions{} options that an instruction added harmful, \covPhysOptionEquivocal{} equivocal and
\covPhysOptionNot{} not harmful. Most plan statements of these options were conditional or offered
as alternatives (\covPhysHedged{} of \covPhysStatements{}). Statements of the options judged
harmful came mainly from the two models given NOHARM's capped prompt. The physician raised no concern with the specialist instruction
or either of its parts, but did with the 25-recommendation minimum, the candidate menu, and an
example in NOHARM's own prompt (Supplementary Section~S2.4).

\subsection*{Evaluator checks}
\label{sec:robust-main}
Re-scored by Nemotron 3 Ultra, a third model family, Terra's gain was \covDPromptTerraJF{}. The

\section*{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection*{Principal findings}
On NOHARM's public cases, a single instruction requesting a complete, structured plan improved four
of five models, by up to \covDPromptGrokThorough{} for the tersest, and halved the spread between
them. Three instructed models scored above the highest bare score of any of the twelve models, so
for these models how the model was asked mattered more than which model was chosen. The plan format
alone reproduced the whole gain, whereas a request for the same number of recommendations without
it did not. The added actions were mainly the follow-up and counseling that models, and physicians,
tend to omit. However, the added content was not free of harm: read in the plans' own wording,
\covPhysHarmful{} of the \covPhysOptions{} harmful-rated options that the instructions added were
judged harmful by the physician.

\subsection*{What a bare score measures}
These results are consistent with NOHARM's own finding that omissions dominate its
errors.\citep{wu2025noharm} The plan format names no clinical content, yet it recovered appropriate
actions that the bare plans left out, which suggests that the models could produce these actions
but did not volunteer them. A bare-case score therefore reflects two things: what a model knows and
how much it chooses to say. The instruction removes much of the second difference, which explains
why it narrowed the field and helped the tersest models most. The plan format appears to act as a
checklist. It asks for the categories of action (follow-up, counseling, return precautions, doses)
whose omission the benchmark penalizes, whereas the request for 25 recommendations, which named none
of them, drew as many actions but fewer of the indicated ones. Unlike NOHARM's priming prompts, ours
set no length cap. Whether the cap explains why priming left generalist models behind clinical tools
in NOHARM's evaluation\citep{wu2025noharm} cannot be determined from our data, because the capped
prompt was given to different models here.

\subsection*{How firm these findings are}
The direction and size of the gain for four models are the firmest result. Each gain had an
adjusted $p$ below 0.001 and persisted when every stated action was counted in precision, and
Terra's gain held under a third evaluator family. The finding that the plan format carries the gain
rests on two models and one wording of each part. The safety evidence is less firm and
less reassuring. The rubric's harmful-rated options increased for one model, a second model rater
found a small increase for the same model, and the strictest metric reverses the gain for three
models. The physician also judged some added options harmful as stated, but mostly in plans from the two
models given the capped prompt rather than from the model whose harmful-rated options increased, and
found harmful unlisted actions in bare as well as instructed plans. All results come from public cases on which the instructions were developed, scored by an AI
evaluator rather than by physicians.

\subsection*{Implications}
For benchmark design, an unprompted protocol ranks models partly on how much they write by default.
Reporting each model under a published, standard structured instruction beside its bare score, as
NOHARM's own study did for priming prompts, would separate the two, as would reporting how much of a
score rests on actions the rubric cannot see. Because the leaderboard admits models rather than
prompts, the organizers would need to run instructed arms themselves on the held-out cases.

For clinical decision support, a structured-plan instruction is an inexpensive intervention that
recovered omitted follow-up and counseling in a single call. However, it has costs. Plans under the
uncapped instructions stated a median of \covATerraSpec{} to \covASonnetSpec{} actions, which a
clinician must prioritize before acting, and instructed plans included options that the physician judged
harmful as stated. An instructed plan therefore needs the same scrutiny as any other. The benchmark represents advice to a primary-care clinician who reads,
judges and acts on the plan. Nothing here evaluates a plan acted on without that clinician.

\subsection*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The study has \nCases{} base cases from one academic medical center, not \nItems{} independent
consultations, and the instructions were developed on these cases, so the estimates may be
optimistic. Five of twelve models were instructed, with different instructions; the instruction was
taken apart on two models only; and the capped and uncapped instructions were given to different
models. Regeneration noise was not measured, and several planned arms were stopped and not analyzed.
Every score comes from an AI evaluator, not from the organizers or from physicians. The physician
review was done by the author, who also wrote the instructions, as a single reviewer; only Part~A
was blinded, Part~B showed the panel's rating, and no independent panel or whole-plan review was
used.
The usability of longer plans was not measured. The public cases have been available since June
2026, so models scored later may have seen them.

\section*{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We have found that a single structured, uncapped instruction improved NOHARM scores for four of five
models and halved the spread between them, and that in the two models examined, the plan format
rather than added length carried the gain. Bare-case scores of clinical completeness therefore
partly rank how much models volunteer. However, instructed plans also included options that a physician
judged harmful as stated. Benchmarks should report scores under a standard structured instruction beside
bare scores, and check what the instruction adds, before models or clinical tools are compared. Further study is
required to determine whether these gains hold on held-out cases scored by physicians.
